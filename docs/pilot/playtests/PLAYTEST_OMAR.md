# Toki's Coaching Quest — playtest diary

**Who I am:** Omar. DLIS at a school in Al Ain since September; six years teaching maths before that. First time opening this. Played on my phone (390×844), English.

**How long:** Worlds 1–3, about 55–60 minutes end to end, reading everything. World 1 took the longest (about 25 min) because I kept getting the risk question wrong. Worlds 2 and 3 took roughly 15–18 min each.

**What I was hoping for:** I've been dreading my first "hard" conversation with a 15-year veteran who runs a beautiful lesson that isn't teaching anything. I wanted to see whether this game would show me *how to say it*, not just *what's wrong*.

**Short version:** The conversations are the best part and the part I actually learned from. The "Assess the Risk" step is the worst part — I got it wrong on the first try in 9 of 13 classrooms and the hints often sent me the wrong way. And the game sometimes tells me the teacher didn't do the thing she just agreed to do, which made me feel cheated.

---

## World 1 — The Busy Classroom

### 1. The Talkative Classroom (English, Ms. Mariam) — ~8 min, score 82

The three clues were clear: groups saying "me too, totally" with nobody asking why; a board that just says "Discuss the chapter"; the teacher saying "Great energy!" and never asking a follow-up. I nearly picked **Collaboration** (because the collaboration is what looks hollow) but went with **Thinking quality** because of "Nobody asks why". Correct first try. Felt good.

Then the risk. I picked **Moderate** — the room is lively, the teacher is kind, it needs attention but it isn't a disaster. Wrong. The hint said: *"how much of what you saw would still be there tomorrow if nothing changed — and how much of it is already working?"* I honestly could not tell which direction that pointed. "Still be there tomorrow" sounds serious; "already working" sounds not serious. I went **High**. Wrong. Same hint again, nothing new. I thought "fine, the world is called 'when engagement replaces learning', this is the lethal mutation thing" and picked **Lethal Mutation**. Wrong. The answer was **Minor** — the last one I'd have chosen, and I got +4 for it after four tries.

I'm not saying Minor is wrong pedagogically — she's one task-wording away from a good lesson, I get it now. But the game had just spent its intro telling me "the most impressive classrooms are not where the deepest learning is happening", so of course I over-rated. And the hint didn't help; it repeated itself identically after each wrong answer. By the third wrong tap I was clicking to get through, not thinking.

Coaching move: I picked **discussion roles (leader, recorder, challenger)** — it felt gentle and "challenger" seemed to fix "nobody asks why". Wrong; the hint ("reread the board — if the task line were different…") was actually good this time and pointed me at **add prompts requiring evidence and justification**. Fair enough.

The conversation: her opening line is "Did you see them? Every single group was talking." I picked **"It was great energy. Collaboration is clearly a strength in this room."** — because that's exactly what I'd say, I'd want her on side first. She said thank you. Then her next line was *"So what, I make them stop agreeing with each other?"* — which came out of nowhere, because I hadn't said anything about agreeing. It felt like she was answering a different Toki. I then picked the "keep every bit of it — what if the task said 'convince the other table'" line and she said she'd try it Thursday.

Reveal said **PARTLY RESTORED**: *"The poster went up. Students read it on the first day and then talked exactly as before."* What poster? I never suggested a poster, and she had just said she'd try "convince the other table" on Thursday. So I lost conversation points and the explanation didn't match what happened on screen. I assumed I was punished for the praise line, but the game never said so.

### 2. The Activity Overload (Science, Mr. Yousef) — ~6 min, score 86

Easy diagnosis: **Completing the activities** (the lesson objective literally says "complete all 5 stations"). Risk: I'd just been burned for over-rating, but this looked worse than classroom 1 — the *objective itself* is about activity. I went **High**. Wrong. Hint: *"Is the missing column a crack, or has the whole floor gone?"* I read that as "whole floor gone" → **Lethal**. Wrong. So **Moderate** (try 3, +8). Two classrooms in and I've learned nothing about *how the game decides* Minor vs Moderate vs High vs Lethal — only that I'm bad at it.

Coaching move: I picked **keep the stations but add a two-minute reflection card** because it keeps what he built. Wrong; hint pointed at Station 3 and I went **reconnect every activity to a clear learning objective**.

Conversation: he says "Tell me honestly — what would you change?" and I said **"Honestly, it was impressive. The time-on-task was excellent."** (Yes, I know.) He said "that's what I'm going for". Then — same as classroom 1 — his next line responded to a question I never asked: *"The plan says 'explore density' for three… They did both."* Then I asked the density question and he admitted "two of them, maybe". **PARTLY RESTORED** again, with an outcome ("objectives printed on the station cards, pace the same") that was a plausible result of my conversation this time. Still no indication of which line cost me.

### 3. The Output Illusion (Art, Ms. Fatima) — ~5 min, score 103

Clearest classroom so far. Student can't explain her own water-cycle poster; rubric has no criterion for explanation. **Demonstration of understanding** first try. I picked **High** and — first try correct. I genuinely don't know why this is High and the Talkative Classroom is Minor; both have students who can't reason about the content. I'd love a sentence on the reveal that says *why* this level and not the next one down.

Coaching: I picked **a rubric with a criterion for scientific accuracy** because the rubric clue screamed it and it's a small ask. Wrong — hint ("which move would make copying impossible rather than checking afterwards?") was good, and I took **embed explanation, reasoning and application into the task**.

Conversation: her wall is her reputation. I picked **"The display is lovely. Can I hear one student explain their panel without reading it?"** then the **QR-code 30-second recorded explanation** idea. She loved it: "that would actually look good. And I'd hear every single one of them." Reveal: **PARTLY RESTORED — "The reflection sheet is still there. Most students still fill it in. The projects are still graded on what they look like."** Reflection sheet? She agreed to QR codes! This is the second time the outcome text describes something that didn't happen in my conversation. At this point I started to distrust the conversation score.

### 4. The Assessment Trap (Maths, Mr. Khalid) — ~5 min, score 125, two badges

My subject. Twelve weeks of colour-coded scores, same students at the bottom in week 3 and week 11, plan written in September and unchanged. **Instructional response to the data**, **Lethal Mutation**, **use the evidence to reteach, regroup, intervene and adjust** — all first try. I'll be honest: I picked Lethal partly because the answers so far had gone Minor → Moderate → High and I thought "it's a ladder". That's a bad reason, and the game shouldn't let me get away with it.

The conversation was the best in World 1. He's tired, "it's all there", and I'd learned by now not to open with praise. I asked about **Question 4 last Tuesday — eleven students wrong the same way — what happened Wednesday?** He said "I didn't go back. I don't have time." Then he pushed: *"You want me to reteach every time eleven kids get something wrong? I'd never finish the syllabus."* That is word for word what Mr. A in my own school said to me last month. The option I chose — **drop one of the three quizzes, spend the saved time on five minutes fixing Q4 — less marking, not more** — and his reply "Nobody has ever suggested I do less" — that is the thing I will steal. I felt like I learned a move, not just that I scored. PRACTICE RESTORED, and the outcome text matched what we agreed.

Badges "Purpose Protector" and "Mutation Hunter". Nice, though the names mean nothing to me yet.

### 5. The Gamification Mutation (Any subject, Ms. Noura) — ~5 min, score 125

The title tells you the risk level ("Mutation"). **Speed and performance**, **Lethal**, **reward reasoning, explanation, reflection and improvement — not speed alone**. The bonus question "why change what's rewarded rather than remove the game?" was good — I actually thought about it.

Conversation: "Did you see Omar? He hates maths and he was first on the buzzer…" (a student called Omar — I laughed). I said **"It's engagement — nobody could say otherwise. Here's what I'm curious about: in round two, who got it right but didn't buzz first?"** She named Hind. Pushback: *"So Hind's slow. What do you want me to do, give her a head start?"* I chose the **one round in three with no buzzer, point goes to the best explanation** option. PRACTICE RESTORED. By now I had figured out the formula: *agree with the true thing, ask about one specific student, then offer one small swap that costs nothing.* The game taught me that formula through the choices themselves, which I appreciate — but it taught it mostly by making the praise-first option always the wrong one, so I learned it as a rule, not as a judgement.

### The Perfect Classroom (final visit) — ~4 min, score 150, "Master DLIS" badge

Six dimensions, each with one line like "Toki reads the objectives and checks them against the lesson. Your call: does this dimension check out?" — and **no evidence to look at**. No clue cards, no screens. I said "it checks out" five times because I had nothing to go on and the intro said everything was flawless. Dimension 6 then said *"Toki does something different now — sits beside the students"*, which was obviously the tell, and I said "the drift is here". Then "Locate the mutation" offered four options when the previous screen had just told me the answer in a paragraph. Then the coaching move, where one option was clearly the only sensible one.

The *idea* — students who have learned to perform learning — is genuinely the most interesting thing in the whole game, and the closing message ("its hardest level had no villain to catch") landed. But I didn't *do* anything to find it. It was a slideshow with buttons. I'd have loved to actually see a student answer that sounds right and isn't.

And then I was given a badge called **"Master DLIS — You see the whole school"** after one world, while World 8 is literally called "The Master DLIS". Felt unearned and a bit confusing.

**World 1 verdict: 3/5.** Good scenarios, two excellent conversations, but the risk step is a guessing game with unhelpful hints, and the conversation outcome text contradicted my choices in 2 of 5 classrooms.

## World 2 — Assessment Under the Microscope

(The map unlocked World 2 by itself once World 1 was complete; I didn't need to touch anything.)

### 1. The Exit Ticket (Science, Mr. Rashid) — ~5 min, score 104

Exit tickets collected daily, rubber-banded, unread; three of five got Q2 wrong the same way; tomorrow's plan moves on. **What the responses changed about the next lesson** — first try. I noticed this is the same drift as the Assessment Trap in World 1 and the Dashboard that's coming next. More on that below.

Risk: I picked **Lethal** (unread for a week = lost its purpose). Hint: *"Is the practice working against learning, or one unread pile away from it?"* I read "one unread pile away from it" as "it's nearly fine" and went **Minor**. Wrong. **Moderate**. Wrong. **High** on the fourth try. So the hint meant "it IS working against learning". The hint was a binary question and I picked the opposite reading of the one intended. That's on the wording, not on me. Four tries again; +4 again.

Coaching: **sort tonight's tickets into three piles; the piles set tomorrow's first task** — first try. Conversation: I'd learned. **"They do — and you didn't. Can I show you one from yesterday? Q2…"** → he hadn't opened them → pushback *"I can't read thirty tickets a night"* → **"Not reading — sorting. Three piles, ninety seconds…"** → "That I can do before I leave the room." PRACTICE RESTORED. Lovely. The 90-seconds-three-piles move is practical enough that I wrote it down.

### 2. Marks Without Feedback (English, Ms. Layla) — ~4 min, score 115

"14/20", one circle, no words; student folds it in eight seconds; same error two weeks later. **What changed in the next piece of writing**. Risk: I said **High**; hint said "the marks are fair and the system runs — what failed in those eight seconds: the marking, or something after it?" I read that as "the marking works, so it's less bad" → **Moderate**, correct on try 2. That hint was actually readable. Coaching: **one named next step instead of the number, then ten minutes to apply it** — first try.

Conversation with a tired teacher with 90 essays: I chose **"Fewer, if anything. I watched Omar get his back: eight seconds, folded, in the bag…"** and then **keep the number in the markbook, one line on the paper, ten minutes Monday to redo the paragraph — same marking time**. "He might, actually." PRACTICE RESTORED. The pushback ("their parents will ask for the number") was real and the answer was the kind of thing I could say tomorrow.

### 3. The Data Dashboard (Maths, Mr. Tariq) — ~4 min, score 115

Strand 3 red for six weeks, flat; plan identical; seating unchanged. **What the dashboard changed in this week's plan**. Risk **Lethal** → hint "is it beyond repair?" → **High**. I'm now reading every hint as "pick the one below what you picked". Coaching: **pair every red cell with a named decision: reteach, regroup, or extend** — first try.

Conversation was the best in World 2. He's defensive — "Everyone who walks in here tells me strand 3 is red." I chose **"I wasn't going to say it — you clearly see it. What I don't know is what the red is: twelve students, or three? And are they sitting together?"** His answer ("Nobody told me to move anyone") is heartbreaking and true. Pushback: *"I can't rebuild the room every time a cell goes red."* My pick: **every red cell gets one word and a name — want to try it on strand 3 now, with me?** → "that's ten minutes of planning." PRACTICE RESTORED and the outcome ("strand 3 turned amber in a fortnight") followed from what we agreed. This is how I'd want every reveal to behave.

### 4. End-of-Unit Success (Science, Ms. Reem) — ~5 min, score 105

86%/90% on a test that is the worksheet with numbers changed; a 90% student can't say why the reaction speeds up. **Whether the understanding survives an unfamiliar question**. Risk: **Lethal** → hint → **High** (try 2). Coaching: **add two unfamiliar-context questions now and revisit in three weeks** — first try.

Conversation: a brand-new teacher with her first good result, "she wants to be told she did it right". I'll admit I picked **"Ninety is wonderful for a first unit. Enjoy it…"** because that is exactly what I'd say to a nervous NQT in her first term, and I wanted to see what the game did with it. She said thank you — and then her next line was *"So the test was too easy? Should I rewrite it?"* — again, responding to a criticism nobody made. I then chose the **kettle / rusting gate two-questions-tomorrow** option and she said **"Yes — that I can do. Can I show you them before I give them?"**

Reveal: **PARTLY RESTORED — "The next unit's test is 'more varied'. This unit closed on Monday at 90%, and Hind still can't say why the reaction speeds up."** That is the outcome of the option I did *not* pick ("make the next unit's test a bit more varied"). She agreed, on screen, to two transfer questions tomorrow. The game then told me she didn't do it. I felt genuinely cheated, and I can't tell whether the game is saying "your praise line poisoned the well" (which would be a legitimate lesson — say it!) or whether it's just a bug.

### World Challenge — The Assessment Mutation — ~5 min, +130, "Coaching Specialist" badge

This format was much better than the Perfect Classroom: five actual scenes with evidence in them, and I had to call each one "pattern" or "healthy". Two of the healthy ones look messy (scrap-paper quiz, abandoned lesson with groaning students) and two of the pattern ones look polished (40-column markbook, warm parent evening). I got 5/5, but I had to think, and the line *"the coach who sees drift everywhere is as blind as the one who sees it nowhere"* is the single most useful sentence in the game for someone like me who is currently terrified of both.

Named the pattern (**assessment has become compliance**), **Lethal** (correct — finally a Lethal that was accepted), systemic move **change the Monday meeting: every number arrives with a named decision for named students**.

**World 2 verdict: 4/5.** The conversations are excellent and I came out with three concrete moves (three piles / one line + ten minutes / a word and a name). Loses a point for the risk step still being a lottery and for the Ms. Reem outcome contradicting my choices. Also, this world and World 1 classroom 4 and World 3 classroom 3 are all the same drift — "you collected data and didn't act on it" — five times by the end of World 3. I was pattern-matching, not observing.

## World 3 — Alef in Action

### 1. 100% Completion (Maths, Mr. Hamad) — ~5 min, score 115

This is my school. 100% completion on the slide at Monday's meeting, 41% on the lesson check, video skipped at 0:04, the teacher only ever opens the completion column. Diagnosis: four options all pointed at one clue each; I went **lesson-check scores and one opened attempt per group** — correct. Risk: my gut said Lethal; the game had trained me over nine classrooms to go one lower, so I said **High**. Wrong — it *was* Lethal. So the game's own pattern made me second-guess a correct instinct. Coaching: **open three attempts with the teacher; let the check scores set tomorrow's plan** — first try.

Conversation: "Which other class is at a hundred?" I picked **"None — yours is the only one, and that took work. Can we open one attempt together? Sara's, maybe."** → he opens it, sees the 0:04 skip → pushback *"If the target was the check score, someone should have said"* (which is a fair point and the game let it be fair) → **open the check column before the completion column tomorrow; same lesson, same target. Does that cost you anything?** → "No. I just never looked at it first." PRACTICE RESTORED, outcome matched. I will use "does that cost you anything?" this week.

### 2. Students on Devices (English, Ms. Shamma) — ~5 min, score 103

Silent room, three different wrong answers submitted without a pause, back-row boy on his fourth read of the same explanation, teacher watching the room not the live view. I picked **what is happening on the screens between a question and an answer** — correct. Risk **High** → hint ("what is missing is one person's attention — how far has that gap travelled?") → **Moderate** (try 2). Coaching: I went for the gentle one, **agree a signal — a red card on the desk**, because it respects her "I don't hover". Wrong — the hint was excellent: *"three of these moves wait for a signal from him. Which reaches him without one?"* → **live view open, circulate with one question, pull two stuck students aside**. That hint taught me something about *why* the gentle move fails: Ahmed will never raise the red card either.

Conversation: she says "they're fourteen, not nine". I said **"Agreed — nobody learns with someone standing over them. Can I show you one student? Back row, Ahmed…"** → *"Where's the line?"* → **"Not all of them — two…office hours."** PRACTICE RESTORED. Her pushback was the most reasonable in the game and the answer respected it.

"Group B weak on fractions — again", three Sundays running; identical plan for all four groups; "assign by group" never used. Wrong — hint: "which move would have stopped the second Sunday note from being written?" → **end every Sunday note with a verb and a group**. Good hint, I accept it.

### 4. AI Gives Every Answer (Arabic, Mr. Nasser) — ~4 min, score 125

Best classroom in the three worlds. ## How the teacher conversations felt

This is where the game earns its keep. Every pushback line was one I have heard or expect to hear: "I'd never finish the syllabus", "their parents will ask for the number", "I can't rebuild the room every time a cell goes red", "they're fourteen, not nine", "if the target was the check score, someone should have said". And every time, the right reply had the same shape:

1. Agree with the thing that is genuinely true.
2. Name one student and one moment ("Question 4 last Tuesday", "Ahmed, back row, four times").
3. Offer one swap that costs the teacher *nothing* or *less* ("drop one quiz", "ninety seconds, three piles", "two students to your desk").
4. End with a question: "Would that work?", "Does that cost you anything?"

I did learn this, and I learned it from the options, not from a lecture. That's the good news.

The bad news is *how* the game taught it: the praise-first option was wrong every single time, and the game never once said "that was the line that lost you the restore". After five classrooms I wasn't choosing "validate + question" because I understood why — I was choosing it because the other two were obviously the Nice One and the Harsh One, and the Nice One always loses. For a player who already over-praises, that's a rule I'll follow in the game and forget in the corridor. Three times (Mariam, Yousef, Reem) the teacher's second line responded to a criticism my Toki never made, which broke the illusion that she was listening to me.

And three times (Mariam's "poster", Fatima's "reflection sheet", Reem's "more varied test") the **What Happened Next** described an outcome from a different branch than the one I played. The teacher said yes on screen; the reveal said she didn't do it. If the message is "your praise opener undermined the agreement", say that in words. Otherwise it reads as the game not tracking what I did.

## What felt unfair

- **The risk step.** Nine first-try misses in thirteen. The four-level scale has no visible rubric, the hints are riddles that repeat verbatim after each wrong tap, and in two classrooms I only reached the answer by elimination (+4 for a fourth try). Worse, after enough classrooms I learned "the game usually wants one lower than my gut" and that *made me get World 3 classroom 1 wrong* when my gut was right. A one-line reason on the reveal — "Minor because the energy and the task structure are already in place; only the task wording is missing" — would turn this from a lottery into a lesson.
- **Outcome text not matching the conversation** (above).
- **Nothing on the hub shows my score per classroom**, just a tick. "Revisit any classroom to sharpen your score" — sharpen from what? I had to remember 82.

## What felt patronising or too easy

- The Perfect Classroom audit: six "does this check out?" screens with no evidence, then a four-option question whose answer was in the previous paragraph. The idea is the best in the game; the execution is a slideshow.
- Classroom titles give away the risk ("The Gamification **Mutation**", "The Assessment **Mutation**") and the subtitle gives away the diagnosis ("PARTICIPATION ≠ LEARNING", "COMPLETED ≠ LEARNED"). I read the subtitle before the evidence every time.
- The "Why is this the right move?" bonus had one obviously-best answer every time. It's a nice reflective beat but it's never a decision.
- Same drift five times across three worlds (data collected, not acted on). By Ms. Maha I was on autopilot.

## UI friction

- The hint appears *below* the four options and was cut off at the bottom of my screen — I had to scroll to read it (screenshots 012, 013). Put it above the options or scroll to it.
- The "Look again" toast flashes and is gone before I've read it.
- The evidence notes drawer is collapsed on the diagnosis screen; I opened it every time. Default it open for the first classroom at least.
- Phone back button: I tapped Badges, then back, and landed on a blank page (about:blank). Progress survived a reload, which is good, but the back button should take me to the map.
- World hub copy says "The Final Visit awaits. When you're ready, enter The Perfect Classroom" in Worlds 2 and 3, where the final level is called "The Assessment Mutation" / "The Digital Illusion".
- Intro screen fades in slowly; for ~2 seconds the classroom art and hotspots aren't tappable and nothing tells you to wait.
- The "Master DLIS" badge after World 1 (see above).
- Otherwise: fast, clean, readable at phone width, the conversation auto-scrolls properly, no text overflows, the classroom illustration is charming. Toki is cute. The Arabic toggle is right there (didn't test it).

## Would I keep playing on my own?

Through World 3, yes — I actually wanted to see the next conversation. Worlds 4–8 I'd do if someone in my PLC was doing them too. On my own, the risk step would slowly grind me down; the fourth time I get +4 for guessing by elimination, I'll stop caring about the score, and the score is the only thing the badges care about.

## What I'd tell my line manager

"It's worth 45 minutes for every new DLIS, specifically for the conversation scripts — I came out with four lines I'll use this term. Don't make anyone chase the badges: the risk-rating step is unreliable and a clean run depends on it. If you can, play World 3 classroom 4 (the AI essay) in the next DLIS meeting and talk about it as a group."

## Three things to fix first

1. **Explain the risk level on the reveal, and make the hint change on the second wrong tap.** One sentence: "Minor, because X is already in place and only Y is missing." Right now the hint repeats itself verbatim and two hints ("crack or whole floor", "working against or one pile away") read as the opposite of what they meant.
2. **Make "What Happened Next" follow what I actually said.** Three of thirteen reveals described an outcome from a branch I didn't take. If the praise opener is what downgrades the restore, say so explicitly on the reveal ("the opening line told her she was right; the second line couldn't undo that").
3. **Give the Perfect Classroom evidence.** Six dimensions with nothing to inspect is a coin toss, and the final reveal is handed to you a screen early. One clue card per dimension — including one that *looks* clean and is — and the "Locate" question becomes a real call.

(Runner-up: stop reusing "data collected, not acted on" five times. And show per-classroom scores on the hub.)
